Why the Cup exists

165 years of the first Tuesday in November

Gold-rush Melbourne wanted a race to rival the great handicaps of England. It got one, and the colony stopped work to watch it.

A gold-rush showpiece

Captain Frederick Standish, a steward of the Victorian Turf Club, is credited with the idea. Seventeen horses ran the first Cup on 7 November 1861 for £710 and a hand-beaten gold watch, watched by 4,000 people.

A holiday by demand

By 1865 public servants and bank staff had a half-day for the Cup and businesses shut at lunch. The day became a public holiday for much of Victoria from the 1870s.

A nation's sweep

Radio from 1925 and television from 1960 carried the race to every state. Workplaces still draw sweeps, and the country pauses at 3pm.

Bringing people together

A Cup for every community

The field itself is a migrant story. Horses arrive from Ireland, Britain, France and Japan, and the 1861 winner came south by steamer. Cup Day can be a shared table for families who are new to Australia and those who have been here for generations.

The free sweep

Draw horses from a hat with no money involved. Children, seniors and people whose faith or budget rules out gambling can all take part on equal terms.

Try thisPrizes are donated: a hamper from a local migrant-owned business, or a donation in the winner's name to a charity of their choice.

A table of many kitchens

Turn the Cup Day lunch into a shared meal where everyone brings a dish from home. Food is the easiest first conversation between neighbours.

Try thisLabel each dish with its name in the original language and the country it comes from, matching the flags of the international runners.

Fashions from every field

Fashions on the Field has been part of the Carnival since 1962. Open your local version to sarees, hanbok, kimono, kente, áo dài and every other kind of formal dress.

Try thisAsk each entrant to share thirty seconds on what their outfit means. The stories become the event.

Watch it in your language

The rules of a handicap race are confusing to anyone seeing it for the first time. A short explainer makes newcomers feel included rather than lost.

Try thisLibraries and community centres screen the race with bilingual volunteers and a one-page guide in the main local community languages.

Journeys, horse and human

International horses spend weeks in quarantine and travel halfway round the world to race here. Many families in the room have made a similar journey.

Try thisPair each international runner with a community member from that country who tells their own arrival story before the race.

Look after each other

Research shows family violence rises around big sporting days, driven by alcohol, gambling losses and pressure to perform masculinity. A good event plans for that.

Try thisRun alcohol-free family zones, check in on neighbours who are alone, and display 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) and Gambling Help beside the screen.
